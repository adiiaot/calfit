## Chapter 1 — Introduction
- **Background.** I have watched many people around me download fitness apps in January and delete them by March. It kept happening, so I started asking why. The answer was rarely laziness. People were trying — their tools were just working against them. That observation is where CalFit started.
- **Real-world problem identified.** From talking to people and watching how they actually track fitness, four patterns kept coming up. First, everything lives in different places: workouts in a notebook, meals in memory, sleep nowhere at all. Nobody can see the full picture, so nobody knows if they are improving. Second, the popular apps are built for foreign users. A Nigerian trying to log eba or ofada rice ends up guessing, because those dishes simply aren't there. Third, every user gets the same generic plan — the beginner and the gym regular are treated as the same person. Fourth, there is no one checking in. One missed week quietly becomes a missed month, then quitting. Put together, the outcome is always the same: invisible progress kills motivation.
- **How the application solves it.** I built CalFit to answer each of those four causes directly. Everything goes into one app — food, water, workouts, steps, sleep, fasting — and one progress screen shows whether you are moving forward. The food search carries Nigerian dishes alongside global ones, and a photo scanner helps users who don't want to type. The AI coach and meal plans respond to your own goal, stats and history instead of handing out one-size-fits-all routines. And partners, shared goals, daily streaks and reminders supply the everyday nudge that stops a missed day from becoming a missed month. Users can also download everything they recorded, because their effort should always belong to them.
- **Problem statement (short version).** Fitness tracking is scattered, foreign-centred and impersonal, so people quit. CalFit unifies tracking, localises food logging, personalises guidance and adds accountability, so users can see progress and stay consistent.
- **Aim.** My aim was simple: one easy app where a person can track food, workouts, sleep and fasting, and get guidance that actually fits them.
- **Specific objectives.** I set out to (1) let users log meals, water and calories; (2) record workouts, steps, sleep and fasting; (3) provide an AI coach for advice and meal plans; (4) let users team up with accountability partners and build streaks; (5) let users download their own data; and (6) make the app installable and testable on Android phones and iPhones.
- **Scope.** This report covers the mobile app and its online database. It doesn't cover Play Store or App Store release — there are no developer accounts yet — and payments are not active.
- **Significance.** Sticking with one app is far easier than juggling five. If tracking feels easy, people keep doing it, and that consistency is the whole game in fitness.
- **Intended users.** Beginners starting out, gym members tracking seriously, and friends who want to motivate each other.

## Chapter 2 — Requirements and Analysis
- **The existing problem.** Before CalFit, a typical person's fitness life looked like this: workouts scribbled in a notebook, calories guessed in the head, sleep not recorded anywhere. Nothing in one place, so progress was impossible to see.
- **The proposed solution.** I proposed one app that keeps every record under the user's own account, shows progress in charts, and answers questions through an AI coach that knows the user's history.
- **Functional requirements (what the app must do).** Sign up and build a profile; log food, water, workouts, steps, sleep and fasting; chat with the AI coach; generate meal plans; add accountability partners and chat with them; view progress charts; keep daily streaks; receive reminders; export personal data; switch between light and dark appearance.
- **Non-functional requirements (how well it must work).** I wanted the app to open fast, survive slow networks gracefully, keep every user's data private to them, and look right on both small and large phones.
- **User workflows (how a person uses it).** A normal day looks like this: open the app, log breakfast, see what's left for the day. Train in the evening, save the session, watch the weekly chart grow. Log sleep before bed. Check in to keep the streak alive. Nothing in that flow takes more than a minute.
- **System constraints.** I had to work within real limits: no Play Store or Apple developer accounts (so no store release), AI answers depending on a third-party service with usage limits, and step counting needing a phone with a motion sensor.

## Chapter 3 — System Design
- **Technology in plain terms.** I wrote the app once and it runs on both Android and iPhone. The screens sit behind a bottom menu everyone already knows how to use. Records live in a secure online database, one account per user, so your data follows you across devices. The smart features — AI chat, meal plans, food photo scanning, voice notes — reach outside services through a secure middleman, which means secret keys never sit inside the app itself.
- **Structure.** Screens (what you see) talk to small helper services (which fetch and save), which talk to the online database and AI services. The flow diagram lives in `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md`.
- **Data kept.** Profiles and goals; food, water, workout, step, sleep and fasting records; notes; chat messages; partner links; reminders. Nothing more than the app needs.
- **Privacy by design.** Every record belongs to one user, and the database only ever shows you your own. Login details are locked in the phone's secure storage, not in plain files.
- **Design decisions I made.** One shared codebase instead of two separate apps — it saves months and kills an entire class of "works on Android but not iPhone" bugs. AI keys on the server side, because anything inside the app can be pulled out of it. Step counting set up once at the top level, so it keeps running no matter which screen you're on.

## Chapter 4 — Implementation
- **How I built it.** I worked on a Windows machine in small, careful steps: read the code, fix one thing, test it, write it down. I tested with Expo Go on a real iPhone and prepared an install file for Android phones.
- **Main features.** Onboarding and profiles; food search with Nigerian dishes included; a photo food scanner; workout timer and history; step counter; sleep log; fasting timer; streaks with celebrations; progress charts; AI coach chat and meal plans; partner chat; personal notes; reminders; data download.
- **Important logic, simply explained.** Calories remaining is just goal minus eaten. Portions scale food values up or down. Steps convert to calories at a fixed rate. Sleep average is the week's total divided by days logged. The fasting timer counts from your start time to your target. Streaks grow by one each new day you check in.
- **What I deliberately left out.** The app doesn't pretend to compute your ideal calorie target with some medical formula — it uses sensible defaults you can change. And I didn't fake anything: payments, for example, are honestly just a placeholder screen for now.
- **Handling mistakes.** Every save is wrapped so a dead network shows you a message instead of crashing. Empty screens tell you what to do next instead of staring back blankly. Deleting anything always asks first.
- **Working on both phone types.** I made the layouts respect notches, home bars and small screens, kept the keyboard from covering buttons, and made sure enlarged text doesn't break anything.

## Chapter 5 — Testing and Evaluation
- **How I tested.** Honestly, in three layers. First I read the whole codebase looking for weak spots. Then I wrote an automatic check script and ran it after every fix. Finally I prepared a step-by-step manual test list for real phones.
- **Test environment.** My Windows computer for the code checks, a real iPhone with Expo Go for live runs, an Android install file for assessor phones, and a live test database with nothing personal in it.
- **What I found and fixed.** The download screen showed wrong sleep numbers — fixed. Fasting and workout downloads were missing details — fixed. The fasting timer broke every time you reopened the app — fixed. The app crashed on launch if setup values were missing — now it shows a clear message instead. The AI provider switched off our model overnight — I found a live replacement and wired it in. The camera crashed if you tapped too fast — now the button waits until the camera is ready. And some privacy wording that wasn't quite true — I rewrote it honestly.
- **Actual results.** All 32 automatic checks pass. The project's health check passes 21 out of 21. The manual phone tests are written out step by step, and I've marked every one honestly — none counts as passed until it actually runs on a device.
- **Remaining limitations.** A few deeper improvements are proposed but not yet applied — tighter AI access control, stricter photo-storage rules, full account deletion. The charts also need redesigning for very long histories. I've written all of this down openly. Hiding weaknesses in a defence would be worse than having them.

## Chapter 6 — Challenges and Lessons
- **Records using old field names.** Some screens asked the database for fields by outdated names and got blank answers back. What I took from it: keep one shared list of field names and re-check them after every database change.
- **An AI model switched off without warning.** One morning the coach just went silent — the provider had retired the model. Lesson learned the hard way: never hard-depend on a single model name. Keep it in settings, watch for shutdown notices, always have a replacement in mind.
- **The app crashed instead of explaining.** A missing setup value killed the whole app on launch. That one stung, because an assessor was holding the phone. Lesson: startup problems must always produce a readable message. Never a crash.
- **Phone differences.** What fit beautifully on a big phone overflowed on a small one, and the keyboard swallowed buttons whole. Lesson: design for the small screen first, and build every form keyboard-safe from day one.
- **What I knew before vs during IT.** I came into this with software planning, design and coding experience, and that's what I used to plan and review this work. My IT placement added something different on top: freelancing, dealing with clients, negotiation, pricing and budgeting. I'm keeping the two separate in this report because blurring them wouldn't be honest.
- **How I used AI tools.** I want to be straight about this: I defined every requirement, chose what to build, and reviewed and tested each change myself. AI coding tools helped me draft and debug faster, but nothing entered this project without my review and a passing test. If it broke, it was my call — and my fix.

## Chapter 7 — Conclusion and Recommendations
